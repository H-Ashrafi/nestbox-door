The one rule

Electricity only flows in a loop. It leaves the battery's + post, goes through the parts, and comes back to the − post. Every wire on this page either carries it out or brings it back. The "bring it back" wires are all called .

The story of one day

How the pieces work together

Read this once and the diagram stops looking like spaghetti. Power flows from left to right. Signals flow from the brain outwards.

  1. The battery pushes

    The pushes at about 12 . The sits in the red lead. If a wire ever shorts, the fuse melts and the battery cannot start a fire.

  2. The 12 V line feeds three things

    After the fuse, the red wire is called the . It feeds the buck, the motor driver's POWER terminal, and the top of the battery sense divider.

  3. The buck makes 5 V for the brain

    The takes 12 V in and gives a steady 5 V out. The XIAO board needs 5 V. Feed it 12 V and it dies instantly.

  4. The brain asks the clock what time it is

    The is a tiny computer. It talks to the over two wires, . From the date and your latitude it works out today's sunrise and sunset.

  5. The brain tells the driver to move

    When it is time, the XIAO raises one of two pins, . The sees that and connects the battery's full 12 V to the , in one direction or the other.

  6. The brain keeps an eye on the battery

    All day, the XIAO peeks at the battery voltage through the . When the battery drops below 11.9 V, the code parks the boxes open and stops moving. A flat battery therefore never locks the hens out.

The three red pins

The chip reads D0, D8 and D9 at the instant it powers up to decide how to boot. A resistor to ground on any of them stops the board from starting. That is why IN1 and IN2 go to D1 and D3 rather than the obvious D0 and D1.

The confusing one

What the battery sense divider is

The XIAO wants to know how full the battery is. The only way it can "see" anything is by measuring a on one of its pins, with a part inside it called the .

There is a catch. The ADC on pin can only measure between 0 and about 2.5 V. The battery sits at 12 to 13 V. Connect the battery straight to D2 and you destroy the chip.

So we shrink the voltage first. Two in a row, one big (220 kΩ) and one small (33 kΩ), split the battery voltage between them like two people sharing a rope. The small one gets a small share: 33 ÷ (220 + 33) = 0.13, about one eighth.

The point where the two resistors meet is the . A wire runs from there to D2. A full battery at 12.6 V shows up at D2 as 12.6 × 0.13 = 1.64 V, which the ADC can read comfortably. The code multiplies the reading back up by 7.7 to get the real battery voltage.

The sits across the small resistor and smooths out flicker, so the reading holds still.

The resistors are huge on purpose. Only about 0.00005 A trickles through them, so the battery does not notice the divider at all.

How to read the coloured bands

Hold the resistor so the gold band sits on the right. The first two bands are digits. The third band says how many zeros to add. The gold band means "accurate to 5%". Your pack may have five bands instead of four. Both versions are shown when you click a resistor.

Cannot tell orange from red under your lamp? Set the multimeter to Ω, touch one probe to each leg, and read the number. That is quicker than squinting.

Skill

Soldering, if you never have

Practise on the spare half of the experimenters board first. Twenty throwaway and you will have it.

  1. Set the iron to 350 °C and wet the sponge

    Do not go hotter. Hotter does not make it faster. Hotter lifts the copper pads off the board.

  2. Tin the tip

    Melt a little solder onto the tip and wipe it on the sponge. It should look bright silver. A dull grey tip transfers almost no heat, so re-tin it every few joints. This is called .

  3. Heat the metal, not the solder

    Press the tip against the pad and the pin at the same time. Count to two.

  4. Feed solder into the joint, not onto the iron

    Touch the solder to the far side of the pin, away from the tip. It should run around the pin and wrap it in half a second.

  5. Take the solder away first, then the iron

    Hold everything still for three seconds. If you move it while it sets, you get a grainy joint that fails in the coop.

  6. Look at it

    A good joint is shiny and cone-shaped, like a tiny volcano hugging the pin. A dull ball sitting on top is a . Reheat it until it collapses and flows.

The four ways this goes wrong

  1. You carry a blob over on the tip. It sticks, it looks finished, and it is not connected.
  2. You sit ten seconds on one pad. The copper lifts off and that board is scrap. If it has not flowed in three seconds, lift the iron, let it cool, and try again.
  3. You bridge two pads. This happens easily on 2.54 mm headers. Check every neighbouring pair with the meter on before you power anything.
  4. You leave no . A wire that flexes at the joint snaps in about a month, usually in the rain.

Order of work

Build it in stages

Never wire the whole thing and then switch it on. Add one board, test it, then add the next. A fault is then always in the thing you just touched.

  1. Solder headers onto all four boards. Do nothing else yet.

    Set the meter to continuity. Check that every pin connects to its own pad and not to its neighbour.

  2. Power the buck on its own

    Connect battery → fuse → VIN and GND. Put the meter across VOUT and GND. It must read 5.0 V ± 0.1. If it reads 12 V, you have VIN and VOUT swapped, and you have just saved your XIAO.

  3. Add the XIAO

    Connect VOUT → 5V and GND → GND. Upload the program. If the light blinks, the power chain works.

  4. Add the clock

    Connect 3V3, GND, SDA and SCL. Print to the . If the time counts up, the two clock wires are right.

  5. Add the divider

    Fit the 220 kΩ, the 33 kΩ and the 100 nF. Compare what prints against the meter on the battery, then set .

  6. Add the driver and actuator, on the bench, not on the door

    Fit both pull-downs. Set to true. Run ten open-and-close cycles before it goes anywhere near the coop.

  7. Now mount it

    Use an under cover with the pointing down. Put the battery in a ventilated crate below the box, on spade terminals.
